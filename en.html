01
Station 1 · the starting point

Shallow or deep foundation?

A structure transfers its loads to the ground through its foundations, so the structure and the soil beneath it interact. Foundation systems fall into two large groups: shallow foundations and deep foundations.

If the bearing stratum is close to the surface, roughly within 1–2 metres (for a building with a basement, close to the superstructure), we design a shallow foundation: the footing transfers the load directly to the bearing stratum. If the bearing stratum lies deeper, the loads are large, or a shallow foundation would settle too much, a deep foundation is the better choice (piles, diaphragm walls, well and caisson foundations). In that case a load-transferring element is inserted between the structure and the bearing ground.

There are two intermediate cases as well. A deepened shallow foundation is built 3–5 m deep, below the load-bearing structure, but in construction terms just like an ordinary shallow foundation; its bearing resistance is also calculated with the assumptions used for shallow foundations (for example, the shaft resistance is neglected). Mixed (hybrid) foundation systems, such as the piled raft, combine shallow and deep foundation techniques.

A shallow foundation can be used if…

  • there is a soil layer of adequate bearing resistance and thickness near the surface;
  • the settlements and other deformations do not exceed the specified limits;
  • the near-surface layer is not very strong, but there is nothing better deeper down either, and the weight of the building can be spread over a large area (raft foundation);
  • the bearing resistance of the ground is low, but the structure is not sensitive to settlement, and a shallow foundation near the surface avoids costly groundwater lowering or deep foundations.

A deep foundation is needed if…

  • a reliable bearing stratum is found only at depth;
  • a shallow foundation would settle unacceptably;
  • a high groundwater level during construction makes a shallow foundation more expensive;
  • water could scour or undermine the ground beneath a shallow foundation, for example at bridge piers in a river;
  • a strongly inclined load could make a shallow foundation slide;
  • it is more economical overall than a shallow foundation.
Conclusion

If the conditions for a shallow foundation are not met, a deep or mixed (combined) foundation follows. The decision is always a technical and an economic question at the same time.

Real examples

MOL Campus, Budapest

The 143 m high-rise has a mixed foundation: a 2.2 m thick base slab on 135 piles, 1.2 m in diameter and 8 m long, standing in the stiff clay marl. The base slab alone took about 4400 m³ of concrete.

Burj Khalifa, Dubai

The world’s tallest building also stands on a piled raft: the 3.7 m thick reinforced concrete raft rests on 192 bored piles, 1.5 m in diameter and about 50 m long.

Family house on good ground

On granular ground of good bearing resistance, the simplest and cheapest solution under a masonry family house is a strip foundation below the frost depth (0.8–1.0 m). A deep foundation would be a needless expense here.

Types and construction of shallow foundations

Strip and pad foundations are the simplest and cheapest. If large differential settlements would develop between columns or walls, or the loads can only be transferred over a large area, ever stiffer systems follow: continuous beam, beam-grid, raft and box foundations. The shell foundation is a special solution that is no longer used.

Strip foundations

Strip foundations provide continuous support for walls. Exceptionally, their width may equal that of the wall above (in old buildings the wall and the footing are often not separate, but made of the same material, most often brick). Because the bearing resistance of the ground is lower than that of the building material, footings usually project beyond the walls as cantilevers; because earthworks are inaccurate, a placing “allowance” of at least 5 cm is also needed on each side.

They can be made of brick, rubble stone, rubble concrete, mass concrete or reinforced concrete. Brick and stone footings are built only exceptionally today, but a few decades ago most were like this – they “turn up” again and again when existing buildings are extended upwards or alongside. For practical reasons, the width (B) and depth (m) of the footing cannot be less than 50 cm.

a) Brick

The widening between the basement wall and the footing width is stepped: in height by one course of bricks, horizontally by whole multiples of half a brick. Under the first course, 5–10 cm of levelling sand or lime mortar is placed, unless the foundation sits on sandy ground that is easy to level.

b) Rubble stone

The principles are similar to those for brick footings. It is built with a constant width or with stepped widening, from solid, frost-resistant stones laid in improved lime masonry mortar of grade H10 or better. A single stone may weigh at most 50 kg. Mixed (brick and stone) masonry is common in old buildings.

c) Rubble concrete

Rubble stone found on site or obtained cheaply is built into the concrete, in a band about half the depth of the footing. This is what is called cyclopean concrete. It spread in the second half of the last century for family houses and holiday homes; today it does not pay because of the labour it needs.

d) Mass concrete

The most common strip foundation. It is built with formwork or cast against the trench sides; formwork is used today only if near-vertical trench sides cannot be dug in cohesionless granular soil – formwork is expensive and slow. Its depth is chosen, in simplified form, from the ratio of projection to depth (k : h): depending on the ground, 1 : 2, 1 : 1.5 or 1 : 1.

e) Reinforced concrete

It is built when a large bearing area is needed but something limits the depth of the footing, for example groundwater or a basement. Reinforcement allows a smaller depth and can be more economical overall: the wider an unreinforced footing, the deeper it would have to be.

f) Footing for a partition wall

Partition walls also need foundations, even though they carry only themselves. There are three solutions: a separate smaller strip footing, a thickened and reinforced floor slab, or (with a precast beam floor) a reinforced concrete beam that “carries” the load across to the strip footings of the main walls running at right angles.

Minimum reinforcement in strip footings too

It is often worth reinforcing a footing minimally even if its depth is not reduced (4 longitudinal bars of at least 12–16 mm diameter, 40×40 links): in a local settlement (e.g. a burst pipe) it can partly even out the differential settlements.

    Strip foundations in practice

    Pad foundations (block foundations)

    Columns and posts sit on pad foundations. The plan shape usually follows the cross-section of the column, most often square, or B/L = 1–3.5. Material and construction are similar to those of strip foundations, but because of the larger, concentrated action they are made of concrete or reinforced concrete, with at least a mesh of reinforcement at the bottom.

    The connection between a cast-in-place reinforced concrete column and its footing can be fixed or pinned. For precast columns, the precast reinforced concrete pocket foundation used to be common: the column was set into the “pocket” of the footing and then fixed by concreting it in. Today the precast column is typically set and fixed in a reinforced concrete pocket neck placed on a cast-in-place pad. For a steel column, holding-down bolts are needed to transfer any tensile forces. The footings of structures with a closed plan, such as water towers and factory chimneys, built as a single block and usually circular in plan, can essentially be regarded as large pad foundations too.

    Column–footing connection

    Continuous beam and beam-grid foundations

    Continuous beam foundation (ground beam)

    Because of weaker ground or for structural reasons, the columns are placed on a strip-like beam – usually heavily reinforced – if unacceptably large differential settlements would develop between the columns in one direction. It is always made of reinforced concrete (it is in bending), and it gives the structure longitudinal stiffness.

    Beam-grid foundation

    If we want to reduce the differential settlement in both directions, we design intersecting reinforced concrete beams, i.e. a beam-grid foundation.

    Raft foundations

    A raft foundation is a continuous reinforced concrete structure running under the whole building, supporting walls and columns alike. It is used when the loads of the structure can only be transferred over the full plan area, because the pressure under the previous types of foundation would exceed the bearing resistance of the ground. Because they are relatively thin, rafts are usually very flexible. For smaller loads, a flat slab of constant thickness is built; under walls and columns it can be stiffened with ribs (downstand or upstand ribs). In framed buildings with larger loads, a “flat-slab” (mushroom) layout is usual to prevent punching. If only additional reinforcement remains where the ribs would be, but the thickness equals that of the ribs, the raft has hidden ribs.

    For a building with a full basement and a high design groundwater level, a reinforced concrete raft (an “inverted floor”) is the right choice in any case.

    Raft foundation variants in section

    A wider view: frost-protected shallow foundation

    In colder climates, the frost-protected shallow foundation works on a principle similar to the ground-bearing slab: vertical insulation is placed alongside the footing and horizontal “wing” insulation outwards, so the heat given off by the heated building stays in the ground and the soil around the foundation does not freeze. The US residential code (IRC R403.3) then allows a foundation depth of only 30–40 cm – on condition that the building is heated in winter too.

    Box foundation and shell foundation

    Box foundation

    Further stiffening is achieved if the whole basement structure is made to act together: the base slab forms a monolithic whole with the basement walls and floor built on it – in effect a box girder. Prefabricated panel buildings can tolerate only small deformations without damage, so this is where it was used most often (a cast-in-place reinforced concrete receiving level).

    Shell foundation

    Special slabs: mathematically defined surfaces of single or double curvature in which only normal forces (tension, compression) develop, no bending. They save material but need a lot of labour and time and strict geometric accuracy; they can be cast in place or precast. They did not prove economical and are no longer used.

    It stands (stood) on a shell foundation: the Körszálló

    Interestingly, the Körszálló hotel on Szilágyi Erzsébet fasor in Budapest also has a shell foundation. Designed by György Szrogh and opened in 1967, the 18-storey, roughly 64 m tall cylindrical block, about 30 m in diameter, was built with slipforming. It closed in 2025; the upper part of the building is being demolished and rebuilt, and according to the plans it will be a residential building by 2028.

    Station 3 · the method

    The design process for shallow foundations

    Before starting on the foundations of a structure, we have to check whether an adequate ground investigation report is available on which the foundations can be designed safely.

    First we try to judge whether the task can be solved with a shallow foundation – or whether it is already clear that a deep foundation is needed. If shallow, can the simplest and cheapest strip and pad foundations be used, or is it clear from the start that a stiffened foundation, such as a beam grid or a raft, will be needed? The initial idea can still be changed later in the design. Foundation design is also a technical and an economic question.

    Reviewing the drawings

    The structural drawings and calculations of the project have to be studied before the design: layout, loads, sensitivity.

    Ground investigation report

    Is the available report adequate for designing the foundations? If not, an additional investigation comes first.

    Technical and economic decision

    The chosen solution has to be both safe and economical – often the construction time decides.

    The design process

    Once we have all the data, the design of the foundations can start. Knowing the structure, its loads and its sensitivity to settlement, as well as the ground and groundwater conditions, the first step is to consider whether some shallow foundation system can be used. If not, we move straight on to deep foundations. If so, we choose the type, set the foundation level, size the foundation area in the ultimate limit state (for a raft it is given anyway), and make a first estimate of the depth too.

    From the dimensions, the stresses and settlements can be calculated. This is the first “check”: are the settlements and differential settlements acceptable? If not, something has to change and we go back; if necessary, we move on to deep foundations. If so, we check the stability of position (sliding, overturning) and the safety against uplift, and finally comes the structural design: choosing the concrete grade and the reinforcement.

    The usual design steps

    1. Choosing the foundation type – based on the layout, loads and sensitivity of the superstructure and the expected settlements, taking the neighbouring structures into account.
    2. Setting the foundation level – taking into account the bearing stratum, the groundwater level, the frost and volume-change limits, the expected footing depth, the neighbouring foundation levels, and undermining, internal erosion and excavation.
    3. Determining the footing width – with verification calculations for safety against bearing failure and for the settlement criteria.
    4. Designing the foundation structure – determining the material, the depth and the reinforcement from the contact pressure, by structural design.
    5. Checking stability and uplift – by examining the equilibrium of the foundation, or the structure, treated as a rigid body.

    Geometry

    04
    Station 4 · the most important decision

    Setting the foundation level

    The foundation level is the lower bearing surface of a shallow foundation, where the loads are transferred to the ground. The depth of foundation is the vertical distance between the foundation level and the final ground surface.

    The whole foundation system depends decisively on the choice of foundation level. Design should always start from the minimum foundation level required by the structure – an unnecessarily deep foundation is expensive (excavation, dewatering, protection of neighbouring buildings). At least the six requirements below have to be met.

    1

    Below the frost depth

    The foundation level should be below the frost depth, otherwise freezing and thawing lift the ground under the foundation and then loosen it.

    2

    On bearing ground

    It should stand on ground that compresses only to an acceptable extent – no harmful settlements should develop. We do not found on topsoil or made ground.

    3

    Above the groundwater if possible

    Working below the groundwater needs dewatering, which is expensive and also affects the surroundings.

    4

    As the structure requires

    It should be as deep as the structure demands – think of a basement or an underground car park.

    5

    Below the desiccation depth

    On ground that changes volume, the foundation level should be below the desiccation depth, i.e. below about 2 m. The details are covered in the module “Foundations on unfavourable ground”.

    6

    Fitting the surroundings

    It should match the foundation level of the adjoining neighbouring buildings.

      The frost depth

      The frost depth is the greatest thickness of the soil layer that cools below 0 °C in winter. In Hungary the frost depth is:

      • 0.8 m in granular soil; below 500 m above sea level it can be reduced to 0.5 m, provided the winter groundwater level comes no closer than 2.0 m to the foundation level;
      • 1.0 m in cohesive soil above 500 m above Baltic Sea level, and 0.8 m below 500 m;
      • 0.5 m for a foundation on solid rock.

      For parts of a building not exposed to frost (e.g. in a basement), a soil cover of at least 0.4 m has to be designed – this is met anyway because of the minimum footing depth of 50 cm.

      Frost-depth calculator and the path of frost in the ground

      Why is frost dangerous in cohesive soil?

      Frost heave is not caused only by the 9 % volume increase of the pore water. In frost-susceptible (silty, clayey) soil, the small pores let capillary flow carry water continuously from deeper water-bearing layers to the descending frost front, where it freezes into ice lenses. The lenses grow on top of each other in layers and lift the ground – and the foundation standing on it. On thawing, soft, water-saturated soil is left where the ice was, and it compresses under the load. Ice lenses need three conditions at once: a descending frost front, a supply of water and frost-susceptible soil.

      Building with a partial basement

      In a building with a partial basement, the base of the higher foundation must be set so that the natural slope line drawn from its inner edge does not cut the line of the basement wall above the basement floor level. If it did, the load of the shallower foundation would act on the basement wall as lateral pressure, and when the basement is excavated the ground could run out from under the foundation. Whenever possible, design a building with a full basement: with a partial basement, the different foundation levels and loads usually cause uneven movements and cracks.

      Slope-line check

      Stepped deepening and sloping ground

      Foundation levels at different heights must be connected by stepped deepening at the perpendicular footings: the line joining the corresponding corners of the footings should not be steeper than 30°. On sloping ground (or with horizontal loads) the stability against sliding must also be considered: never design an inclined foundation level, always step it, and a single step should be no higher than 50 cm.

      Step planner

        Next to a neighbouring building

        If we have to build right next to a neighbouring building, two rules decide: the foundation level of the new building must not be above the old one, and has to be deepened if necessary; and if the deeper floor of the new building puts its foundation level below that of the old building, the old foundation has to be underpinned with concrete, or strengthened with jet grouting.

        Station 5 · what has to be verified

        Limit states and design methods

        When designing shallow foundations, we first have to review which ultimate and serviceability limit states must be checked and which requirements must be met. A limit state is the state beyond which the foundation no longer fulfils the design requirements.

        Ultimate limit states (ULS)

        Click the pictures: each one plays how the foundation fails in that limit state.

        The four (five) acronyms

        GEO – failure or excessive deformation of the ground, where the strength of the soil or rock is significant in providing resistance. STR – internal failure or excessive deformation of structural elements, where the strength of the structural materials governs the resistance. EQU – loss of static equilibrium: a rapid and significant change of position as a rigid body, in which the strengths play no significant part. UPL – loss of equilibrium of the structure or the ground due to uplift. (The Eurocode knows a fifth one too: HYD, hydraulic heave.) For shallow foundations, the GEO and STR limit states are the ones most often checked.

        Serviceability limit states (SLS)

        The most important requirement is adequate safety against bearing failure of the ground beneath the foundation (GEO): in the failure mechanism, large displacements develop before failure is reached, and they soon damage the structure. In most cases, however, it is not the bearing resistance but limiting the settlements and differential settlements that is the biggest problem: foundations must be designed not only for bearing resistance but also to limit settlement. Move the slider and watch what happens to the building as the movements grow!

        Movement ladder – from cosmetic damage to structural damage

        illustrative

        The structural adequacy of the foundation (STR) means that it can take the bending and shear actions on it, i.e. its size and strength (reinforcement) are adequate – for bending, shear and punching. For structures with a high centre of gravity and for larger horizontal forces (e.g. retaining walls), the stability of position must also be ensured against overturning (toppling) and sliding (displacement). For lighter structures reaching well below the groundwater level (pools, underpasses, underground car parks), there is a risk of uplift – often only during construction.

        Two famous cases

        GEO

        Transcona, Canada, 1913

        On first filling, a deep bearing failure developed in the clay beneath the raft of the grain elevator: the contact pressure was about 297 kPa, close to the ultimate pressure of 307 kPa calculated with the classical theory. In 1914 the elevator was jacked back upright on 14 piers taken down to rock – one of the most important case histories confirming bearing resistance theory.

        SLS

        Pisa, Italy

        Construction of the tower, which stands on a masonry foundation 19.6 m in diameter and at most 5.5 m deep, began in 1173; it tilted because of the uneven compression of the soft clay. The structure stands, but its serviceability is impaired. Between 1999 and 2001, soil was carefully extracted from beneath the north side, reducing the tilt by about half a degree.

        Design methods

        According to Section 6.4 of Eurocode 7-1, one of three design methods can be used for shallow foundations:

        • direct method – a separate calculation model for each limit state. For ultimate limit states, the assumed failure mechanism is modelled as accurately as possible, with an ultimate-pressure formula or numerical (finite element) design; serviceability is checked with a settlement calculation based on elasticity theory;
        • indirect method – based on comparable experience and field or laboratory measurements or observations, related to the loads of the serviceability limit state, so that the requirements of all relevant limit states are met (e.g. bearing resistance estimated from a penetration test with an empirical formula). Its advantage is less calculation; it is used in Geotechnical Category 1 and partly in Category 2;
        • prescriptive method – a presumed bearing resistance is used, mainly for foundations on rock: an allowable contact pressure can be taken from the type, discontinuities and uniaxial compressive strength of the rock, with guidance in Annex G.

        In the Foundation Engineering course we deal in more detail only with the direct method.

        The bearing failure mechanism of shallow foundations

        When a gradually increasing load acts on a foundation, the ground beneath it compresses more and more. Three stages can be distinguished on the load–settlement curve.

        I.

        Elastic range

        At first the settlement is proportional to the load; under constant load the settlement stops (ds/dt = 0).

        II.

        Transitional range

        The penetration starts to grow rapidly. Both vertical and horizontal grain movements occur, and plastic zones form under the edges of the foundation (ds/dt = constant).

        III.

        Plastic range

        Bearing failure develops along a distinct slip surface: the loaded area loses its support, sudden settlement occurs, and the ground heaves beside the footing (ds/dt → ∞).

        “Failure” does not always happen in the same way. According to model tests (e.g. Vesić), three cases can be distinguished from the shape of the settlement curve:

        • general shear failure,
        • local shear failure,
        • punching shear failure.

        Which one dominates depends on the dimensions of the foundation and their ratio, the type of soil (granular or cohesive), and the density or stiffness of the soil. In the lab below you can rotate all three, and use the sliders to find out what makes one turn into another.

        Failure mechanism lab

        Soil
        Footing

        General shear failure

        ID > 0.70

        In dense granular soil a complete failure surface develops, running out to the surface, and the ground heaves. Under usual conditions this is what to expect, on one side of the footing. The load–settlement curve has a distinct peak, at a settlement of about 0.05B.

        Local shear failure

        0.35 < ID < 0.70

        In medium-dense granular soil the failure surface develops only partly, and heave does not necessarily occur. Under larger reinforced concrete rafts and towers, a deep slip surface cannot form, because settlements of decimetres destroy the structure first. Instead, local shear failure may occur under the edges, mainly in softer ground. Typical settlement: 0.07–0.15B.

        Punching shear failure

        ID < 0.35

        In loose granular and soft cohesive soil there is no failure surface, at most a depression of the ground surface; slip surfaces in the sense of soil mechanics no longer apply here. This is also how piles are pushed into the ground. Typical settlement: 0.15–0.20B.

        The density index

        ID = (emax − e) / (emax − emin)

        where e is the current void ratio of the soil, and emax and emin are the void ratios of the loosest and densest states that can be produced in the laboratory. With increasing depth of foundation the behaviour shifts towards punching: with a deeper foundation the same sand is more likely to punch in.

        The history of the mechanism in brief

        The failure mechanism under a rigid, rough footing was first described by Prandtl (1920) for the plastic indentation of metals; Terzaghi applied it to strip foundations in 1943 – this became the bearing resistance formula. Vesić distinguished the three failure modes from model tests in the 1960s, and in 1973 he showed in a chart which one to expect depending on the relative density and the relative depth (D/B*, where B* = 2BL/(B + L)). According to the textbooks, for a shallow foundation the ultimate load develops at a relative settlement (s/B) of about 4–10 % in general shear failure, and about 15–25 % in local shear and punching.
